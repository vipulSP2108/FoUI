## 1. Core Pedagogical Objective
The central pedagogical goal of the FUX course project is to practice:
1. **Observation:** Uncovering implicit user practices, physical affordances, breakdowns, and cognitive friction in real-world contexts.
2. **Ideation:** Envisioning novel, user-centered physical, visual, or computational artifacts to mitigate identified problems.
3. **Preliminary Design & Iterative Refinement:** Translating insights into structured design specifications, interaction workflows, and minimal intervention prototypes.

---

## 2. Project Directions Defined by Course Guidelines

The course guidelines specify two possible directions for the final project:

### Direction (a): Critical Assessment of an Existing Artifact
* Pick an already developed artifact.
* Explore its use and engage with **REAL users** in their authentic working environment.
* Grasp the **mental models** in operation.
* Identify operational problems, ergonomic breakdowns, and cognitive failures.
* Suggest evolutions and design alternatives.
* Play with the artifact yourselves and maintain an honest, critical stance regarding the User Experience (UX).

### Direction (b): Design, Prototyping & Cyclic Refinement
* Consider a well-defined domain problem.
* Propose a new, user-centered design and articulate its theoretical and practical rationale.
* Build a minimal prototype (physical mockup, software interface, or hardware demonstrator).
* Conduct a simple evaluation with representative users to illustrate at least one cycle of UX refinement.

### Group 9's Integrated Strategy: The Dual-Direction Synthesis
Rather than selecting only one narrow pathway, Group 9 executes an integrated synthesis of both directions:
* **Empirical Direction (a) Component:** Conducting a comparative longitudinal study of the **renovated IITGN Medical Centre** against historical baseline data from the previous facility setup.
* **Evolutionary Direction (b) Component:** Proposing, detailing, and evaluating the **AI-Powered Adaptive Medical Waste System (AI-AMWS)** with physical visual cues and predictive capacity rebalancing.

## 3. The Five Formal Assessment Criteria

The submitted 10-page report will be graded strictly according to the five criteria established by Prof. Nuno Guimarães:

### Criterion 1: Clarity of Objectives
* Clear, unambiguous articulation of the project scope.
* Explicit statement of whether the report focuses on critical analysis of an existing artifact, design of a new one, or a comparative evolution.
* Clearly framed Research Questions (RQs) and operational hypotheses.

### Criterion 2: Methodological Soundness
* Demonstrated rigor in empirical data gathering (contextual inquiry, ethnographic shadowing, structured/semi-structured interviews, environmental luminance and contrast audits).
* Methodological transparency in recording waste generation variables, observer protocols, and user interaction breakdowns.
* Traceable transition from raw observational qualitative/quantitative data to analytical synthesis (affinity diagrams, user journey maps).

### Criterion 3: Critical Assessment
* Depth and honesty in evaluating the current artifact/environment.
* Avoiding surface-level praise; actively probing breakdowns, slips, mistakes, latent organizational failures, and physical ergonomic mismatch.
* Unbiased evaluation of the proposed design's feasibility, potential trade-offs, edge cases, and limitations.

### Criterion 4: Bibliographic References
* Scholarly grounding in foundational Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and UX literature (e.g., Don Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things*, Jens Rasmussen's *SRK Framework*, James Reason's *Human Error*).
* Compliance with Indian regulatory standards (CPCB Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules 2016) and World Health Organization (WHO) healthcare safety guidelines.
* Formal academic citations formatted cleanly in IEEE or ACM style within the LaTeX `.bib` file.

### Criterion 5: Originality and Autonomous Reflection
* Autonomous framing and intellectual ownership of all report narrative, empirical observations, and proposed design solutions.
* Authentic self-reflection on what the group learned through the experience ("The Attentive Engineer" perspective).
* Critical discussion of methodological constraints, unexpected field surprises, and what the team would execute differently in subsequent design cycles.
